# RESEARCH: Did You Know That? No. 05 · "Food" (v2 — diet, health, lifespan)

**v2, 2026-10-13, after David's note:** "focus of what people eat in every century, how healthy and how food and
development made us live longer." Every era now carries three sourced beats: **what people ate** (a real day's or week's
plate), **how healthy it was** (deficiency disease, hunger, height), and **how long people lived** (life expectancy at
birth, always hedged for child deaths, with adult survival where sourced). Facts from v1 that no longer serve the spine
(Naples tomato sauce, Appert, Haber's name, the US food-budget guess) are dropped or demoted to "not used".

projections; mean male height: NCD-RisC 2016; daily calorie supply: FAO), IPCC AR6 SYR SPM §A.2.4 (PDF), the 2025
EAT-Lancet Summary Report (PDF), the Fitzwilliam Museum "Feast & Fast" page, the James Lind Library article on Takaki,
Wikipedia "Takaki Kanehiro", the ICAR interview with M. S. Swaminathan, OWID's synthetic-fertiliser article. NIH's
Goldberger exhibit and the Lancet/IPCC web pages returned 403; their content is taken from abstracts, mirrors and
institutional summaries, marked as such. Story Council **not** run (paid seat). No paid API, no voice.

Status key (as in No. 04): **verified** = primary or institutional text/data read · **corroborated** = two or more
independent secondary sources agree · **hedged** = sources disagree or qualify it, and the script carries that hedge ·
**RE-CHECK** = re-open before voicing · **not used** = checked and left out.

**Life-expectancy rule for the whole script:** every figure is *life expectancy at birth*, an average pulled down by
infant and child deaths. The 1500 era says so explicitly ("Mostly because so many children died. If you made it to
thirty…") and the on-screen figure always carries `at birth · average`. The answer attributes gains to food **and**
clean water, sanitation, vaccines and medicine, and names the McKeown dispute.

## Viewer prior vs. claim
- **Prior 1:** "We live longer today mainly because we eat better." · **Claim:** food mattered (enough calories, the
  nutrients that ended scurvy, beriberi and pellagra, taller bodies), but historians dispute McKeown's food-first thesis;
  clean water alone explains nearly half the fall in big-city US deaths 1900–1936 (F36, F37).
- **Prior 2:** "Hunger is the food problem." · **Claim:** about one in five deaths worldwide is now linked to diet, mostly
  too much salt and too few whole grains and fruit (F2), and in most countries more adults live with obesity than are
  underweight (F34).
- **The guess:** "In 1800 a newborn anywhere could expect ~29 years. Today?" → ~73 (F3, F4).

## Myths and disputes, and how the script handles each
| Popular claim | What the evidence says | Script handling |
|---|---|---|
| "People in the past died at 35." | Averages at birth were low because of child deaths; adults often reached their 50s–60s. | Said in 1500 (F8); every screen figure labelled "at birth · average". |
| "Better food is why we live longer" (McKeown). | Disputed: sanitation, clean water, vaccines, medicine did much of the work (Szreter; Cutler & Miller); nutrition is one interacting factor (Harris). | Reveal names McKeown, says "Many historians disagree", gives the clean-water study (F36, F37). |
| "Lind ran the first clinical trial." | Commonly said; contested. | Not claimed. |
| "Lind proved vitamin C cures scurvy." | He didn't know why; his preserved juice lost potency. | "The right food can cure you" only (F10, F11). |
| "Takaki discovered vitamins." | Right cure, wrong theory; vitamins named later. | "Decades later, it gets a name" (F16, F17). |
| "Pellagra was cured by flour enrichment alone." | Enrichment plus incomes and diet diversity (Clay et al.). | Names all three (F23). |
| "Poor diet kills more than smoking." | Not confirmed in the sources read. | Not used. |
| "Medieval people drank beer because water was unsafe." | Disputed. | Beer is said only as an everyday drink, no reason given (F6). |
