# Claim ledger: the testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006)

- **Date checked:** 2026-10-09
- **Story:** one adult documentary segment on the testing effect, centred on Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255.
- **Method:** Each source was downloaded with `curl` and its text extracted with `pdftotext` (raw and `-layout` modes). Every number below was read from that extracted text. Page numbers are the journal's printed page numbers. Table 2 was checked in layout mode to confirm which ratings belong to which condition. The Adesope et al. (2017) full text could not be opened, so only its abstract is used. That abstract came from the Crossref API record for the DOI. Nothing here is cited from memory. Quotes are 12 words or fewer; everything else is paraphrase with a location.
- **Units:** In R&K (2006), "recall" always means the **mean proportion of idea units recalled** on a free-recall test with no feedback. Each passage was scored out of 30 idea units, and the paper reports results as percentages. Effect sizes in Rowland (2014) are Hedges' *g* (testing vs. restudy, random-effects).

## Story spine supported by the evidence

1. **Case:** In 2006, Roediger and Karpicke gave Washington University students short texts about the Sun and sea otters. Some students re-read them; others wrote down what they remembered (R1a–R1c).
2. **Puzzle:** Five minutes later, re-reading looked better (81 vs 75; 83 vs 71). The students who read most also felt the most confident (R1e, R2d, R3).
3. **Evidence:** After a week, the order reversed. Repeated testers recalled 61% vs 40%, even though they had read the passage only about 3.4 times vs 14.2 (R2c, R2e). In Exp. 1 the result was 56 vs 42 (R1g).
4. **Metacognitive twist:** The group most sure it would remember (4.8 of 7) remembered least (R3).
5. **Mechanism (offered, not proven):** Retrieval practises the skill a later test needs, and the effort is a "desirable difficulty". This was not just re-exposure, since no feedback was given (R4a, R4b).
6. **Robustness:** A meta-analysis of 159 comparisons found an average g = 0.50 favouring testing. The effect is larger with feedback and over longer delays, and a 2017 review agrees on direction (R5a, R5b, R5d, R5g).
7. **Limitation:** The original was a lab study of undergraduates, with two short passages and delays of up to one week. Without feedback and with low initial recall, the benefit vanished (R4c, R5c, R5f).
8. **Meaning:** Testing is a way to learn, not only to measure. Try recalling, check your answers, and come back to it, with no guarantees (R7).
