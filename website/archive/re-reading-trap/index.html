The Re-Reading Trap

Why does the study method that feels best lose to the one that feels worse?

A 2006 experiment, what it measured, how it is explained, and what it cannot tell us.

The story

In 2006, two memory researchers at Washington University in St. Louis, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, asked a simple question: is a test only a way to measure what you know, or does taking one change what you remember?

Undergraduates read short science passages, one about the Sun and one about sea otters, each a little over 250 words. Some students kept re-reading. Others read once, then wrote down everything they could remember. Nobody told them what they got right.

Five minutes later, re-reading looked like the smart choice. The students who re-read were also the most confident they would remember. Then the researchers waited a week.

Your prediction

In the second experiment, 180 students learned one passage in one of three ways. After one week, which group remembered the most?

The evidence dossier

  • MeasuredThe students who re-read went through the text about 14 times; the students who mostly recalled read it about 3.4 times. A week later the recall group still remembered more.Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Table 1 and p. 252. Reading counts are the students' own tallies.
  • MeasuredThe re-readers rated themselves most likely to remember the passage a week later (4.8 on a 1–7 scale, against 4.0) and remembered the least.Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Table 2. A group comparison, not each person's own prediction.
  • MeasuredIn the first experiment, with 120 students, one recall test beat re-reading after two days (68% vs 54%) and after a week (56% vs 42%).Roediger & Karpicke (2006), p. 251.
  • MeasuredA 2014 review of 159 comparisons from 61 studies found that testing beat re-studying on average (g = 0.50). About one comparison in five went the other way. The benefit was larger when learners checked their answers and when the wait was a day or more.Rowland (2014), Psychological Bulletin, pp. 12–15.
  • InterpretationRecalling may practise exactly what a later test asks you to do: pulling an idea out of memory. The effort itself may help, which psychologists call a "desirable difficulty". These are explanations the authors offer; this study did not test them.Roediger & Karpicke (2006), p. 254.
  • InterpretationBecause the tests gave no feedback, the authors conclude that the advantage did not come from seeing the material again.Roediger & Karpicke (2006), pp. 251 and 253.
  • LimitThis was a lab study of undergraduates aged 18–24 at one university: two short texts, written recall, and one week at most. It did not test children, classrooms, skills or longer gaps. In the second experiment, three recall tests did not reliably beat one (61% vs 56%).Roediger & Karpicke (2006), pp. 250–254.
  • LimitWhen learners recalled half or less on the practice test and never checked their answers, the benefit disappeared.Rowland (2014), p. 15.

The takeaway

Re-reading feels like learning. Recalling feels harder, and that feeling is misleading.

Next time, close the book and try to say it back, then check what you missed. It works best when you can already recall a fair amount. It is not a guarantee: this routine draws on the studies above rather than being tested in this exact form.
