## 0:25 – 1:20 · THE LEVER
**Visual:** Split screen: a 1950s slot machine lever / a thumb pulling down a feed to refresh.
**🎵 Music:** minimal synth arpeggio with a casino-like shimmer.
**🔊 SFX:** slot-machine lever pull + reel spin, matched to the thumb's pull-to-refresh · coin rattle on rewards · notification 'ping' layered and pitched down.
**VO:**
> Pull-to-refresh was invented by Loren Brichter for a Twitter app. He later pointed out something unsettling: slot machines would be far less addictive if gamblers **didn't get to pull the lever themselves**.
> Your thumb is the lever. The reward is unpredictable. And unpredictable rewards are the strongest habit-builders psychology has ever measured.
> The result: the average internet user is now online for about **6 hours 38 minutes a day**.

**Source cards:** Raskin estimate (reported by The Times / BBC); Brichter interview (The Guardian); DataReportal *Digital 2025*.

## 1:20 – 2:20 · FRANKLIN'S EXPERIMENT
**Visual:** Franklin's 1789 codicil (parchment), coins multiplying across a 200-year timeline; Boston and Philadelphia diverge.
**🎵 Music:** 18th-century harpsichord motif morphing into modern strings as the timeline runs.
**🔊 SFX:** quill scratching · wax seal press · coins multiplying (rising chime cascade) · a 'split' whoosh when Boston and Philadelphia diverge.
**VO:**
> Benjamin Franklin ran the most patient experiment in history. When he died in 1790, he left **£1,000** each to Boston and Philadelphia, with one rule: let it compound, untouched, for **200 years**.
> By 1990, Boston's share had grown to about **five million dollars**. Philadelphia, managed differently, ended near two million.
> Same starting sum. Same time. A different system. That's the whole secret of compounding, and it applies to minutes exactly as it applies to money.

## 2:35 – 3:40 · THE TESTING EFFECT
**Visual:** Two groups of animated students; memory bars drain over a week. One bar holds.
**🎵 Music:** curious pizzicato + soft piano; drops out at the flip.
**🔊 SFX:** page-turn rustle (re-readers) · pencil scribble (self-testers) · clock fast-forward 'one week later' · reveal 'thunk' on 61% vs 40%.
**VO:**
> In 2006, psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke split students into two groups. One re-read a passage several times. The other read it once, then **tested themselves**.
> Five minutes later, the re-readers looked smarter. One week later, the result flipped: the self-testers remembered about **61%**. The re-readers? About **40%**.
> Re-reading feels like learning. Retrieval *is* learning. That's why every story in this game ends with questions.

**Source card:** Roediger & Karpicke, *Psychological Science*, 2006.
