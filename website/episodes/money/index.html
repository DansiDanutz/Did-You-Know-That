    How we saw money

    One rung per century: how people saw money — in the episode’s own words. Pick a year.

    1. 1500Something useful
    2. 1661A promise on paper
    3. 1720A belief
    4. 1821As good as gold
    5. 1971Just trust
    6. TodayNumbers on a screen
    7. 2100A promise in code?

    1500 · You wake up: Tenochtitlan

    Something useful

    “Something useful. Something you could even drink.”

    Canoes bump along the canals. The market’s already loud, and it smells of chilli and wood smoke.

    1661 · You wake up: Stockholm

    A promise on paper

    “A promise. Written on paper.”

    The harbour’s frozen, and a sledge scrapes past your window, loaded with copper.

    1720 · You wake up: Paris

    A belief

    “A belief. It’s worth whatever everyone believes it’s worth. Until they don’t.”

    The street smells of horses, and everyone’s talking about paper.

    1821 · You wake up: London

    As good as gold

    “As good as gold. A promise you can swap for metal.”

    Coal smoke, wet cobbles, and a queue outside the Bank of England.

    1971 · You wake up: A suburb of Pittsburgh

    Just trust

    “A promise backed by trust. Trust in the people who run it.”

    It’s a Sunday evening in August, and the whole family’s in front of the TV.

    Today · You wake up: Nairobi

    Numbers on a screen

    “Numbers on a screen. You almost never touch it.”

    Matatus honk on the road outside, and your phone buzzes. Your sister just sent you money. It took seconds.

    2100 · Prediction · a scenario, not a fact

    A promise in code?

    “In 1500, money grew on trees. In 2100, it might be written in code — but it would still be a promise.”

    That morning hasn’t happened yet. But we can follow the evidence.

    About this episode

    Seven mornings, seven kinds of money: cacao beans in Tenochtitlan’s market (and the sellers who faked them), Sweden’s 20-kilo copper coin and Europe’s first real banknotes, John Law’s Paris and the Mississippi bubble, the Bank of England’s printed promise, the August evening in 1971 when the dollar’s link to gold was cut, mobile money in Nairobi — and a 2100 scenario, clearly labelled, where money might be written in code.
